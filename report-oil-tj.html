Waste Oils — Tajikistan

Generation, Pollution Load, Damage Assessment and Short-Term Solutions.

Prepared byEcoAudit-Central Asia
Date15 November 2026
StatusPreliminary
For Internal Official Use Only

1. Basis and Objective

This report has been prepared on the basis of a scientific study by the Technological University of Tajikistan (2022) with reference to the Agency on Statistics; national regulations on hydrocarbon-containing waste; international reference emission coefficients; and international damage valuations.

Objective: to provide an order of magnitude of pollution load and damage where official calculations are absent, and to propose short-term solutions.

2. Generation Volume

According to the study by the Technological University of Tajikistan (2022), the country annually imports 350,000–512,000 tonnes of petroleum products. Technical oils and lubricants account for more than 80,000 tonnes. During use, more than 55,000 tonnes of waste petroleum products enter the environment.

This is a confirmed scientific estimate with reference to the Agency on Statistics under the President of the Republic of Tajikistan — a more reliable basis than a hypothesis.

3. Working Balance Hypothesis

Since the flow structure (burning/dumping/collection) is unknown, the hypothesis 50% burning / 30% dumping / 20% unaccounted collection is adopted as an assumption:

Dumping into soil/water30%16,500

Caveat: The hypothesis is an assumption, not confirmed statistics.

5. Soil Load

Dumping of 16,500 t/year ≈ ~18,300 m³ of liquid hydrocarbons entering soil and ground. One litre of waste oil can contaminate thousands of cubic metres of water — the potential volume of water contamination is orders of magnitude larger than the dump volume itself.

Dumping (16,500 t): cannot be determined without groundwater monitoring data. The lower bound based on soil treatment cost (~$1/t) gives only ~$16,500, which is clearly underestimated.

7. Regulatory Framework

Waste Management Rules (Resolution No. 224 of 02.05.2019) regulate the handling of hydrocarbon-containing waste. An important technical point: the rules allow preparation of hydrocarbon-containing waste for use as fuel if there is an experimental-industrial regulation approved by the authorised body and a positive conclusion of the state environmental expertise. This means that burning is not fully prohibited, but regulated — unlike Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan.

8. Infrastructure Gap

A 2015 study stated that the republic lacks companies for the collection and processing of vehicle waste, including waste oils. No more recent data on the creation of such a system has been found. Scientific research focuses on using local bentonite clays as sorbents for oil regeneration — an attempt to find a cheap local solution instead of imported technologies.

9. Short-Term Solutions

9.1. Biodiesel production with pre-treatment

Waste oil, after pre-treatment (filtration, removal of water and mechanical impurities, neutralisation), can be processed into biodiesel.

9.3. Environmental effect

Processing ~8,400 t/year of waste oil instead of burning/dumping reduces PM/PM-10, NOx, SO₂ and CO₂ emissions from burning; prevents ~8,400 t of oil from entering soil and water; reduces the load on groundwater in areas of mass oil generation.

Note: 8,400 t is about 15% of total generation (55,000 t) and about 30% of the burning flow (27,500 t).

10. Recommendations to Companies (Short-Term, 0–12 Months)

  1. Boiler houses and industrial facilities: install dust and gas cleaning filters on existing boilers; stop burning waste oil without an approved regulation; conclude contracts for waste oil removal with licensed collectors.
  2. Collectors and processors: organise oil receiving points in major cities; use local bentonite clays for pre-treatment before processing; launch pilot mini-plants (1 t/day) in 2–3 regions to test the scheme.
  3. Service and transport companies: hand over waste oil only under contract, with transfer acts; keep records of oil generation and transfer.
  4. Investors and mini-plant operators: replicate modular units according to the scheme; ensure pre-treatment of feedstock for stable biodiesel yield.

11. Limitations

  1. The calculation is not official and cannot be used for legal claims.
  2. The flow structure (50/30/20) is an assumption, not confirmed statistics.
  3. Specific emissions are taken from international reference books, not from measurements.
  4. Damage from dumping into groundwater has not been calculated due to lack of monitoring data.
  5. Burning is regulated, not prohibited — this complicates the assessment of the "illegal" flow.
  6. The scheme of 24 units × 1 t/day is illustrative; actual capacity depends on collection logistics and feedstock quality.

12. Next Steps (Internal)

  1. Request from the authorised bodies actual collection volumes for 2020–2025 to verify the balance.
  2. Initiate field measurements of emissions from boilers (PM, NOx, SO₂, Pb).
  3. Conduct soil and groundwater sampling in areas of mass oil generation.
  4. Launch a pilot mini-plant (1 t/day) and measure actual biodiesel yield after pre-treatment.
  5. After obtaining data — recalculate the report according to national methodologies.
